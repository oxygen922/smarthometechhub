## Introduction

The smart kitchen has finally grown up. ## Top 3 Product Recommendations

### 1. Best Overall: **Thermomix TM8 Smart Kitchen Hub** — ~$1,699

The Thermomix has always been the Swiss Army knife of the kitchen, and the 2026 TM8 is the first model that fully earns its "smart" label. The headline upgrade is the new Cookidoo AI Coach, which uses a built-in camera and weight sensors to recognize ingredients as you add them and suggest adjustments mid-recipe—"your sauce is reducing too fast, lower heat by 20%." In our testing, it was startlingly accurate with sauces, doughs, and custards.

Hardware-wise, you get a 10-inch tilt display, a 2.2L bowl with improved heating (up to 320°F), and a redesigned blade system that handles everything from smoothies to kneading. Native Matter support means it pairs directly with Apple Home, Google Home, and SmartThings—no proprietary bridge required. It also exposes energy usage data to your smart home dashboard, which is a nice touch for the efficiency-minded.

**Where it falls short:** The price. At ~$1,699, it's a serious investment, and the Cookidoo subscription ($69/year after the first year) is effectively mandatory for the AI features. The learning curve is real—plan on two weeks before you're fluent.

**Verdict:** If you cook from scratch more than four nights a week, the TM8 replaces a blender, food processor, sous-vide circulator, and slow cooker while genuinely improving your results. It's the single most capable smart kitchen appliance on the market in 2026.

### 2. Best Smart Oven: **June Oven Gen 4** — ~$1,299

The June Oven has been the benchmark for smart ovens since 2018, and the Gen 4 finally fixes the two biggest complaints about its predecessor: interior capacity and app lag. The new 1.2 cu. ft. cavity fits a full sheet pan (barely, but it fits), and the on-device processor is fast enough that the food-recognition camera identifies what you've put in within about two seconds—we tested pizza, salmon, frozen fries, and a whole chicken, and it nailed all four.

The real magic is the cook-assist workflow: the oven suggests a cook program based on what it sees, you confirm, and it handles temperature and time with a probe for proteins. Results were consistently better than our gas oven on everything except high-heat searing, where the 500°F max is a limitation.

Smart home integration is where the Gen 4 shines. It supports Matter, works natively with Alexa and Google Assistant, and—crucially—supports remote preheat and cook monitoring, so you can start dinner from the car. It also plays nicely with energy-management platforms like OhmConnect, shifting preheat cycles to off-peak windows if you opt in.

**Where it falls short:** It's a countertop appliance, not a range replacement, and at ~$1,299 it costs as much as a mid-range wall oven. The steam-clean cycle is also slower than advertised.

**Verdict:** The best smart oven for households that want foolproof results without babysitting. If you have the counter space and the budget, it's a genuine kitchen upgrade.

### 3. Best Budget Smart Appliance: **Instant Pot Smart Pro 8QT (2026 Edition)** — ~$179

Not every smart appliance needs to cost four figures. The 2026 Instant Pot Smart Pro is the best value in connected cooking: 8-quart capacity, 13 one-touch programs, and a rebuilt app that finally supports Matter, meaning it shows up in Apple Home and Google Home alongside your lights and thermostat. You can start a pressure cook remotely, monitor progress, and get a phone alert when it's done—the three features that matter most.

The new "Recipe Sync" feature pulls recipes from the app and auto-sets time and pressure, which removes the guesswork that trips up new pressure-cooker users. In testing, it held pressure reliably and the sauté function was hot enough to actually brown meat—a common weak point on budget multi-cookers.

**Where it falls short:** The app is still clunkier than Thermomix's, and the AI features are minimal. It's a smart appliance in the "remote control and notifications" sense, not the "AI chef" sense.

**Verdict:** The entry point into smart cooking. If you're smart-kitchen-curious but not ready to spend $1,000+, start here.

## Key Features to Consider

Before you buy any smart kitchen appliance in 2026, evaluate these five factors:

**1. Protocol support (Matter is the baseline).** Matter 1.4 is now the standard, and any appliance without it is a future headache. Matter-native devices work across Apple Home, Google Home, Amazon Alexa, and SmartThings without proprietary hubs. If a product only supports its own app, treat that as a red flag.

**2. Local vs. cloud processing.** Appliances that rely entirely on cloud servers for core functions (like food recognition) can lose features if the manufacturer pulls support or your internet drops. Prefer devices with on-device processing for cooking logic, with cloud as a bonus.

**3. Subscription dependence.** Several smart appliances gate their best features behind monthly fees. Factor the 3-year subscription cost into your budget—a $179 appliance with a $10/month subscription costs $539 over three years.

**4. Energy monitoring and scheduling.** The best 2026 appliances expose real-time energy data and support off-peak scheduling. This matters more than most buyers realize—smart scheduling on an oven or multi-cooker can cut its energy cost by 15–25% depending on your utility's time-of-use rates.

**5. Firmware update history.** Check how often the manufacturer ships updates. A smart appliance is only as good as its software support; brands with a two-year update track record are safer bets than first-generation entrants.

## Buying Guide

### Who should buy what

**Buy the Thermomix TM8 if:** You cook most nights, want one appliance to replace several, and value guided cooking over free-form technique. It's ideal for busy families and anyone who follows recipes closely.

**Buy the June Oven Gen 4 if:** Your pain point is oven results—burnt edges, undercooked centers, guessing at times. It's best for households that roast and bake frequently and want remote monitoring.

**Buy the Instant Pot Smart Pro if:** You're building a smart kitchen on a budget, or you want connected cooking without a subscription. It's the right first step.

**Skip all three if:** You rarely cook, or your kitchen lacks counter space. A smart appliance you don't use is just an expensive gadget—and a smart kitchen only pays off when the appliances get daily use.

### Budget considerations

Plan for total cost of ownership, not sticker price. Add subscription fees (Thermomix: ~$69/year), replacement parts (June's filters and probes run $40–80/year), and consider utility rebates—many US utilities now offer $50–150 rebates for Matter-certified, energy-monitoring appliances. Check your utility's marketplace before buying; it can offset 10% of the cost.

## Installation & Smart Home Integration

None of these appliances require professional installation—all three are countertop units that plug into a standard 120V outlet. Setup takes 15–30 minutes each.

**Integration steps that matter:**

1. **Update firmware first.** Connect to Wi-Fi and run the manufacturer's update before pairing with your smart home platform. Matter pairing failures are almost always caused by outdated firmware.
2. **Pair via Matter, not the proprietary app.** In Apple Home or Google Home, use "Add Accessory" and scan the Matter QR code. This gives you cross-platform control and future-proofs the device.
3. **Build automations around notifications, not just control.** The most useful integrations are alerts—"oven preheated," "pressure cook complete"—delivered to your phone or smart speaker. Voice control of cooking appliances is convenient but secondary.
4. **Set energy schedules where supported.** If your utility offers time-of-use rates, schedule preheats and cooks for off-peak windows via the app or your home hub.
5. **Keep a fallback.** Ensure each appliance works manually without Wi-Fi. All three reviewed here do—confirm this before buying any smart appliance, because cloud outages happen.

One caveat: if you have an older mesh Wi-Fi system, put kitchen appliances on a 2.4GHz band. All three units struggled with 5GHz-only networks during testing.
